<CaseStudySection id="context" title="An AI tutor at classroom scale" eyebrow="Context">

Flexi is CK-12's AI tutor. It sits inside a K-12 learning platform serving
20M+ users worldwide, used in real classrooms — which means its users are not
a single group with a single goal.
Students use it to get unstuck. Teachers depend on the learning still
happening. Institutions need the whole thing to be safe, private, and
observable.

Designing it was less a question of making the AI capable and more a question
of deciding what it should refuse to do.

</CaseStudySection>

<CaseStudySection id="tension" title="When giving the user what they ask for is the wrong answer" eyebrow="The tension">

> What should AI do when giving the user exactly what they ask for undermines
> the user's actual goal?

A student asking for the answer to problem seven wants the answer to problem
seven. Providing it satisfies the request completely and defeats the purpose
of the exercise. Withholding it entirely is unhelpful and drives the student to
a tool that will simply comply.

The product has to hold three needs at once, and they do not naturally agree.

<CaseStudySection id="research" title="Research" eyebrow="Research">

Research is the backbone of this project rather than a preliminary phase. The
questions it had to answer were behavioural: what students actually do when a
tutor will not simply comply, where comprehension quietly breaks down, and what
teachers need to see in order to trust the tool in their classroom.

The areas studied included reading level and age-appropriate tone,
accessibility, academic integrity and over-reliance, points of confusion,
teacher visibility, and the tutor's failure modes.

<CaseStudySection id="decision-scaffolding" title="The answer is not the end" eyebrow="Decision 01">

The first decision was to treat a response as the middle of an interaction
rather than the end of one. Answering is where a search engine stops; a tutor
has to find out whether anything was actually understood.

So the loop continues past the response: check understanding, adapt the level
of support based on what comes back, then stretch slightly beyond it. The
tutor either continues the loop or hands off when it is no longer the right
help.

<CaseStudySection id="decision-uncertainty" title="Expose uncertainty rather than hide it" eyebrow="Decision 02">

A tutor that is confidently wrong is worse than one that is visibly unsure. A
student cannot calibrate their trust against a system that sounds identical
whether it is right or guessing — and in a subject they are still learning,
they have no independent way to tell.

So the design surfaces the model's limits instead of smoothing over them, and
treats recovery as a designed path rather than an error state.

<CaseStudySection id="decision-teacher" title="The teacher is a first-class user" eyebrow="Decision 03">

The decision that changed the product's shape most was treating the teacher as
a user rather than an administrator.

Once teacher visibility is a designed surface rather than a reporting
afterthought, Flexi stops being a chatbot a student talks to privately and
becomes a multi-sided learning system: what happens in a tutoring session
informs what the teacher does next with that student.

<CaseStudySection id="system" title="System" eyebrow="System">

Holding the three needs together is a system problem, not a prompt problem. The
tutoring loop, the uncertainty behaviour, and teacher visibility are the three
mechanisms that keep student help, learning integrity, and institutional trust
from trading off against each other.
