<CaseStudySection id="context" title="The problem: context that never carries" eyebrow="Context">

A job search is not one task. It is jobs, company research, contacts, resumes,
application materials, interview preparation, tasks, and — increasingly — AI
conversations. Each of those lives in a different tool, and none of those tools
know anything about the others.

The cost is not that any single tool is bad. It is that the job seeker becomes
the integration layer. Every move between tools means re-establishing the same
context by hand: who this company is, what the role asks for, what was already
said, which version of the resume went out, what still needs doing.

That work is invisible, repetitive, and paid for entirely in the applicant's
time — at the exact moment they have least of it to spare.

<CaseStudySection id="system" title="The product model: every opportunity is a workspace" eyebrow="Product model">

The central decision was to stop treating a job as a row in a list.

> Make every opportunity a persistent workspace.

An opportunity is the unit the whole product is organised around. Everything
that accumulates during a search — the role, the company, the people, the
research, the materials, the conversation — belongs to it and stays with it.
Nothing has to be re-supplied to do the next piece of work.

LUMO, the AI layer, is not a separate chatbot bolted onto that structure. It
operates across it. Because the workspace already holds the context, LUMO can
act with knowledge of the specific role, company, and history rather than
asking the user to paste it in again.

</CaseStudySection>

<CaseStudySection id="decision-risk" title="Protect the user's effort" eyebrow="Decision 01">

Tailoring an application is expensive. It takes real attention, and job seekers
have a finite amount of it. Spending that attention on an opportunity that was
never real is worse than doing nothing.

So the system evaluates before it generates:

> Do not burn a job seeker's time generating tailored materials before the
> system has evaluated whether an opportunity appears worth pursuing.

This is a gate, not a warning banner. Role intelligence and a risk assessment
run first; if an opportunity looks like a ghost listing or otherwise low value,
the product says so before the user invests in it.

The interesting part is what this implies about the product's stance: it is
willing to talk a user out of work. A tool measured on output volume would
never do this.

</CaseStudySection>

<CaseStudySection id="decision-control" title="AI acts with visible control" eyebrow="Decision 02">

The AI in Offboard does not only answer — it acts. It can research, draft,
revise, and update the workspace. That makes the question of control a design
problem rather than a technical one.

The pattern is that consequential actions are always previewed and always
confirmed. The user sees which tool is about to run, on what, and what it will
produce, before it happens — and sees the result afterwards as a discrete,
attributable change rather than a silent mutation.

<CaseStudySection id="decision-context" title="Context compounds" eyebrow="Decision 03">

Most tools restart. Each new task begins from an empty box, and the user pays
the setup cost again.

Offboard is built so that work accumulates instead. The research done for an
opportunity feeds the resume tailored to it; the resume and the research feed
the outreach; all of it feeds interview preparation. Each artifact inherits
what came before rather than asking for it again.
