<CaseStudySection id="premise" title="The shift starts here. What does the next operator inherit?" eyebrow="Premise">

At 19:00 one operator goes home and another takes responsibility for the building. What changed? What was investigated and deliberately left open? What must the new operator not touch, because someone already decided otherwise?

Neuron already holds the telemetry, topology, incidents, and procedures. The gap is not data. Most systems keep what happened and lose why a person chose what they chose.

> Do not only preserve knowledge for later search. Put the right knowledge into the exact moment responsibility changes hands.

The same applies to the model. Neuron proposes, the operator decides, and the decision and its reason become the record the next shift inherits.

### Who it is for

A critical facilities technician or shift engineer, arriving rather than leaving, with a building to answer for. Handover happens twice a day and is the most repeated moment in the operation. The window is short, and the valuable part is what was left alone: the costliest mistake is undoing a decision a colleague made on purpose.

This profile comes from public material and reasoning, not interviews.

<CaseStudySection id="chat" title="Chat is the right fallback and the wrong default" eyebrow="Position on chat">

A chat box asks the operator for the question at the moment they have the least time and the least certainty about what to ask. Its answer does not attach to an asset, does not persist, and cannot be inherited. So every AI surface in the prototype follows three rules:

- **It attaches to objects.** Selecting an asset is the query.
- **Its output is interface state.** "What happens if this fails" is answered by drawing on the graph.
- **What you accept or override joins the record.** That makes AI reasoning inheritable.

Chat is still good at the question nobody anticipated. A command bar that returns a reconfigured interface instead of a paragraph would keep that strength. I scoped it out.

</CaseStudySection>

<CaseStudySection id="decisions" title="The calls, and what each one argues against" eyebrow="Design decisions">

**Autonomy is shown, not implied.** Every recommendation lists what Neuron does alone, what needs approval, and what it must never touch. A promise about behavior builds more trust than a confidence score.

**Friction scales with reversibility.** Raising an alert threshold is one click. Transferring critical load takes four checks and the asset name typed back. A one-click approve on a consequential action invites rubber stamping.

<ArtifactFrame marks={[{ x: 3.8, y: 55.7, w: 92.4, h: 32.4, label: "Friction scales with reversibility" }]} src="/work/neuron-shift/gate.webp" alt="The decision dialog for transferring critical load. **Approve and Reject is the wrong pair.** The choices are Approve, Defer with a trigger, Approve with changes, and Reject. Deferring names what brings it back. Overriding requires a reason. In operations the common right answer is "not yet", and an override is an expert disagreeing with the model, which is exactly the knowledge the industry is losing.

**The learning layer is not the product.** An earlier version taught the domain inside the operator tool. Real operators know what a PDU is. The teaching now lives in a separate layer, off by default.

<CaseStudySection id="color" title="One meaning per color" eyebrow="Color semantics">

The chrome carries no hue at all. Color only ever means state. Green is flowing or decided. Amber is a condition to watch, a state of the equipment. Red means a person owes a decision, a state of the work, not the plant; deciding clears it.

The proof case is Rack R-42. It sits at 92 percent of its budget and stays amber, because nobody owes a decision on it. The canvas shows where judgment is outstanding, not just where the plant is unhappy.

<CaseStudySection id="unguided" title="Designing for a visitor nobody walks through it" eyebrow="The unguided visitor">

The first version assumed I would present it. In a portfolio, nobody does. A visitor arrives from a link, has a couple of minutes, and does not know the domain. So the prototype makes its own argument: on a first visit, a guided tour covers the core ideas in seven steps.

- **Every step asks for a real action.** A tour that describes buttons is a slideshow; one that asks you to press them lets the product demonstrate the claim.
- **Steps complete on state, not on clicks.** Exploring ahead is never punished.
- **"Do it for me", with one exception.** Every task can be performed for you, except the consequential approval.
- **Phones get their own page.** The console needs a laptop-sized screen, so a phone shows the idea and what the colors mean instead of a squeezed layout.

<CaseStudySection id="technical" title="Where the engineering went" eyebrow="Building the prototype">

React 19, TypeScript, Vite, and React Flow. The interesting parts are not the stack.

**Impact is computed, not written.** The preview removes the selected asset and reruns reachability from the utility source. Unreachable means it loses power; still reachable downstream means redundancy holds it. It is a simplified topology, not an engineering simulation, and it does not model generators.

**Never ship an affordance you cannot honor.** The nodes looked draggable but every drag was discarded. I found the cause in the graph library's source, then confirmed it by dragging a node and measuring that it had not moved.

**Accessibility is part of the work.** Nodes are keyboard selectable, dialogs trap focus, continuous motion stops under the reduced-motion preference, and every colored state is also named in text.

**How it was built.** I built it with an AI pair. I wrote the plans and acceptance criteria, gated each phase, checked every result in a real browser, and made every product and design call. The AI wrote most of the code. A second pass redesigned it for a visitor on their own rather than an interviewer with me in the room.

</CaseStudySection>

<CaseStudySection id="limits" title="The next test needs a real operator" eyebrow="Limits and next steps">

Every value is simulated, and the interface says so. No model is called; reasoning and confidence figures are written and labeled illustrative. Decisions persist in the browser only. I do not claim to know data center operations. I claim to have learned enough to build something an expert can correct.

What comes next, in order:

1. Put it in front of one real operator. Six fields felt right to me. Nobody who runs a facility has told me so.
2. An authoring flow for the outgoing operator. How do you get a tired operator at 18:55 to write one honest sentence about why?
3. A decision timeline that shows model confidence and human decisions moving together.

An open question sits behind the work: could an intelligence layer carry the newest person on site through a failure? My working answer is to hand capability downward in proportion to reversibility, and to aim for a competent first responder rather than a novice hero.
